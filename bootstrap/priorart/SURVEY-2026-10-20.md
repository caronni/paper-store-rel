## Lens: size-coding

## Size-coding compressors (where the decoder's own size counts)

**How claims are marked.** VERIFIED means I checked the claim against a primary source I fetched in this pass: the project's source code, README or changelog, the author's own page, or a release page. VERIFIED (secondary) means a source I fetched that is not the author's own, for example a reverse-engineering write-up the author later corrected. UNVERIFIED means memory or a search-result snippet only. I downloaded no software and ran none. I did read some source files as text through `gh api`.

**What cm2 is, for reference.** I read `bootstrap/typed/cm2_dec.py` (v2) so the transfer notes below are concrete:
- Nine contiguous orders: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 16 bytes.
- The context key is `h & mask`, where `h` holds the whole history as one integer.
- The weight of model w is a shift of `w`, plus a boost when the other count is zero.
- After each bit, the count of the bit seen goes up by one and the other count becomes `min(c, c//4 + 2)`.
- The coder is a big-integer rANS with no renormalisation, primed with the decoder's own text.

That is the Crinkler model family, with fixed weights and fixed contiguous masks.

### Notes per work

- **Crinkler** is cm2's direct ancestor. cm2 v2 already uses Crinkler's mixing: integer count sums with a shift as the weight, and a boost when one count is zero. Two Crinkler features cm2 does not have:
  - **Masks are chosen, not fixed.** The compressor starts from 256 byte masks, walks them in bit-mirrored order, keeps a mask only if it shrinks the output, then prunes masks that stopped helping (VERIFIED (secondary), code4k, quoting Blueberry).
  - **Weights are chosen per section.** All the weights are stored in one 32-bit word.

  The ergon table shows a model of this family landing within about 3.6% of paq8l on a 10 KB input, before counting the decoder. For a 4k payload this family is close to the practical ceiling.
- **kkrunchy** shows what changes once the decoder may be about 1.5 KB and the payload is around 64 KB: a PAQ7-style neural mixer, SSE/APM stages, and above all a **long-match model** plus a domain transform. Giesen's constraint was decode time (a few seconds) and memory, not bytes. That is the same trade-off our ~10 MB archive faces.
- **oneKpaq** shows that switching to logistic mixing still fits in 128–165 B of x86. It also shows that searching for hours to days is acceptable on the compressor side.
- **Shrinkler and upkr** are LZ with adaptive binary contexts. Literals carry no previous-byte context; the LZ part captures repetition. upkr's C reference unpacker is about 60 lines (VERIFIED). It is the smallest complete adaptive LZ+rANS decoder in a high-level language that I found.
- **Squishy** builds its packer as a bootstrap chain: a tiny LZ+arithmetic stage unpacks the strong CM decoder. That is exactly our pattern of a typed loader writing `r.py`.
- **PackFire** sits at the other extreme: a cut-down LZMA, or a 152-byte LZ. It confirms that below about 1 KB, decoder bytes outweigh model strength.
- **Roadroller** is the most relevant high-level-language example. Its whole decoder is one generated JS statement, and the parameters chosen per payload are typed into it as literals:
  - the selector list as a digit string;
  - precision, learning rate, count cap and base count as numbers.

  The README warns that `Math.exp` and `Math.log` are implementation-approximated, so decoders may differ across engines. The safety rests on all browsers sharing fdlibm (VERIFIED). The rANS coder emits symbols directly in the transport alphabet (6 bits per character), so no base conversion is needed. Two weaknesses are stated: long-distance duplication (it recommends an outer Deflate), and inputs under 10 KB, which need faster adaptation.
- **pnginator and JsExe** use a decoder that is already present in the environment (the browser's PNG/Deflate) at the cost of a small bootstrap. Geelnard's numbers price the alternative: a hand-written Deflate decoder in JS is about 4.9 KB, an LZ decoder about 450 B.
- **ZPAQ** is the general end of the range: the model is a program carried in the archive. Crinkler and Roadroller sit in the middle: a fixed decoder plus a few bytes of model parameters.

### What this means for us (ranked)

1. **Let the encoder choose cm2's masks and type them as data.** This applies to the typed stage, R2 and the archive.
   - cm2 already computes keys as `h & q` over the whole history integer, so any sparse mask costs no extra code. Today the list is `256**o - 1` (contiguous orders). Replacing it with a typed list of hex masks, such as `0xFF00FF` or `0xFFFF0000`, is the Crinkler/Roadroller move.
   - Roadroller's default list contains sparse contexts (the second-last byte alone; patterns that skip a byte) alongside orders 0–3.
   - The search is free at encode time: greedy add-and-prune as in Crinkler, or simulated annealing as in Roadroller.
   - Break-even must be measured, not assumed. Nine masks of up to 8 hex digits, say, are a few dozen typed characters; the masks win only if they save more base-32 payload than that.
2. **Type the weights as data too.** Replace `w` from `enumerate` with a typed list of small integer shifts per model. This is Crinkler's per-section weights, stored there as one 32-bit word. Again it costs a few characters of code and a short data line. Crinkler's weight search gives "a few bytes" at 4k scale (VERYSLOW vs SLOW, VERIFIED). Measure it on cm2's own payload before adopting.
3. **Keep the typed stage integer-exact.** Do not adopt Roadroller's floating-point logistic mixer there. Its own README names the implementation-approximated `exp`/`log` as a cross-implementation risk. Python's `math` module inherits the platform's libm, and a deep-time re-typist may use another language. cm2's count mixing is the right family for a decoder that must be exact on any machine. If logistic mixing is wanted later (R2 or archive), use integer stretch/squash tables, as the PAQ family does (UNVERIFIED for any specific table), never `exp`/`log`.
4. **Treat the update rule as a searchable constant, aimed at faster learning on small inputs.** Roadroller's ~3% from its base-count parameter (VERIFIED) and its advice to try smaller learning rates under 10 KB both point at adaptation speed on small inputs. On a ~4.5 KB payload, cm2's `c//4 + 2` decay and the zero-count boost are exactly such constants. Roadroller's capped-count adaptive-rate counter (cap 5) is an integer-friendly alternative worth one A/B test.
5. **Add a match or LZ component for R2 and the archive, not for the typed stage.** Order-k CM misses long-range duplication; Roadroller admits it, and kkrunchy and lpaq add a match model for exactly that reason. For a ~10 MB tar of source trees, one match-model input to the mixer, or an upkr-style LZ+rANS front stage, is the classic gain. Check first whether `strong2` already has one.
6. **Abbreviations and a "state" context for source text** (R2 reader and archive). Roadroller replaces common identifiers and keywords with unused bytes, and adds an "inside a string" flag to every context hash. For Python source, a keyword dictionary plus a string/comment flag is the cheap analogue of kkrunchy's x86 transform, which was worth about 20% there.
7. **The two-stage bootstrap is validated practice.** Squishy and Crinkler's tiny-header modes both do it, and we already do: the typed loader emits the stronger reader. The lesson is to budget the typed decoder by size and the second stage by strength.
8. **Emit symbols directly in the transport alphabet.** Roadroller's rANS emits 6-bit symbols straight into its string, renormalising one character at a time, so it needs no big integers. cm2 v2's single big integer is simpler to type. A streaming radix-32 rANS is the alternative if decode time on long R2 streams ever matters.
9. **Lowest priority: a decoder already in the environment.** pnginator and JsExe rely on the host's Deflate. In Python that would be `zlib`/`lzma`. The R3 rulings chose import-free code, so this is listed only to record that the size-coding community's cheapest decoder is the host's own.
10. **Licences do not block any of the above.** The ideas are free to reimplement. If text is ever borrowed: Roadroller's emitted decoders are public domain, upkr is Unlicense, kkrunchy is public domain, Crinkler is zlib, oneKpaq is BSD-2, and Shrinkler's decompressor is free for any legal use.

**Payload regime against our three stages:**
- **Typed stage** (~4.5 KB payload plus decoder text): the Crinkler/oneKpaq 4k regime. Items 1–4 apply.
- **R2 stage** (10–50 KB): Roadroller's design point (30 KB) and Shrinkler/upkr territory. Items 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6 apply.
- **Archive** (~10 MB): kkrunchy, lpaq and ZPAQ territory, where decode time and memory bind, not decoder size. Items 5 and 6 apply.

### Per-work notes

**1. ZPAQ.** The format defines only decompression, and each block names its own model. A block header carries the table sizes, a list of 1–255 predictive components (CONST, CM, ICM, MATCH, AVG, MIX2, MIX, ISSE, SSE), and up to two ZPAQL programs. HCOMP computes contexts after each byte; PCOMP post-processes the output. ZPAQL is a small sandboxed register machine (A–D, R0–R255, arrays H and M sized in the header). A better compressor never needs a new reader, because the algorithm is "saved in the archive block header" (libzpaq.h). Every version reads archives back to v1.00 (2009). On non-x86 hosts ZPAQL is interpreted, about 2× slower than JIT; that figure is from Mahoney's page. libzpaq is public domain. VERIFIED.

**2. VXA.** This is a modified ZIP. Known methods keep their standard tags so ordinary unzip still works, and an extension header points each file to a decoder stored in the archive. Decoders are 32-bit x86 programs with five virtual system calls (read, write, exit, setperm, done) and stdin/stdout only. Measured: compressed decoder sizes 26.2 KB (zlib) to 129.7 KB (vorbis), slowdown up to 11% against native x86-32. Ford's argument for real x86 over a custom machine is that "instruction encodings are historically more durable than data encodings". He also expects future hosts to run the decoders under emulators such as QEMU. Stated limits: the encoders are not archived, the scheme needs an x86 or emulator host, and decoders that are seldom used can hide bugs until the day they are needed. VERIFIED.

**3. Micr'Olonys.** This is the closest prior art to `bootstrap/`. It stores digital files as 2D barcodes ("emblems") on microfilm or paper, with a self-contained human-readable bootstrap of about a dozen pages including a two-page algorithm. Restoration needs a general-purpose computer and a scanner. The decoders are archived on the medium and run under layered emulation; per the ruling I did not examine that machine further. The arXiv paper (Appuswamy & Joguin, "Universal Layout Emulation for Long-Term Database Archival") frames this as archiving decoders alongside data. The title, authors and abstract are VERIFIED. Two figures come from search snippets only and are UNVERIFIED: a 16 mm microfilm test (a 102 KB image in three emblems, recovered without error) and an estimate of about 1.3 GB per 66 m reel. The full text was not readable here: the PDF would not render, and the PHAIDRA and OSF hosts are behind bot walls or JavaScript.

**4. Piql.** Data is written as dense 2D-barcode frames on silver-halide polyester film. Piql's decoding library "boxing" is C99 under GPLv3, with a commercial licence offered. It bundles glib, Reed–Solomon, LDPC and BCH, so the film decoder is an FEC stack of the same kind as ours (VERIFIED, GitHub). Two claims are trade-press only and UNVERIFIED: that "the reader is open source and stored on the media itself", and the figure of about 2 MB per frame. Piql's Notion decoding guide and its AWA page could not be read (empty render, 404).

**5. GitHub Arctic Code Vault.** The Guide describes 188 reels: one human-readable "guide reel" and the rest software reels, 65,000 frames each. The README and the archive site say 186 reels, so the record disagrees with itself; both counts are VERIFIED as stated. Each reel opens in this order:
- a Zero Reference Frame (a solid black rectangle in one corner of an otherwise empty frame);
- a human-readable Control Frame;
- a table of contents with frame and byte locations;
- the Representation Information, 15 listed items: the frame format, an "unboxing library" description and its source, the ASCII and C specifications, TAR and PDF source, and the XZ specification.

The payload is QR-style frames, then XZ, then TAR, checked by SHA-1. The Guide builds up from what binary and files are. It is frank that decoding by hand is impractical and that a working computer would be needed. So the decoder reaches the reader as C source plus the C language spec: the reader has to bring or rebuild a C toolchain. VERIFIED. The Guide does not say whether the Representation Information is printed as text or encoded.

**6. Bootstrappable builds.** The stated goal is to minimise bootstrap binaries (bootstrappable.org, VERIFIED). stage0-posix builds hex0 → hex1 → hex2 → M0 → cc_* → M2-Planet → mescc-tools "from a single 256 byte seed", and its phase 0 rebuilds hex0 from the seed to check the seed (VERIFIED). The original stage0 began with "a 280 byte Hex monitor" (VERIFIED). live-bootstrap continues the chain to GCC without pre-generated configure scripts or parsers (VERIFIED). Two other figures are UNVERIFIED: 357 bytes in an older live-bootstrap README, and 181 bytes in StageX's documentation. The seed size depends on architecture and version, so cite it as "a few hundred bytes" unless one figure is pinned.

**7. Rosetta Disk.** The disk works the opposite way: no decoder, only optics. Over 13,000 pages on more than 1,500 languages are electroformed in nickel. The outer text starts at eye-readable size and shrinks, which itself tells the reader to magnify. Pages are visible at 100X and readable at 650X. A glass hemisphere doubles as a 6X viewer, and a steel ribbon carries a caretaker log. VERIFIED.

**8. Lunar Library.** The tiers go analog first, then digital. The front cover holds about 1,500 pages readable at 100X; three analog layers hold about 20,000 page images each at 1000X. A picture-and-word Primer is followed by the "specifications for extracting the file formats", and behind those sit 21 nickel DVD masters (about 100 GB compressed). VERIFIED, from the vendor's own page.

**9. Voyager cover.** The cover is a decoding manual in pictograms. Binary-coded timings use the hydrogen transition as the unit. Raster diagrams give 512 vertical lines of about 8 ms each. The first picture is a circle, so the reader can check the aspect ratio and the decode itself. VERIFIED-2nd (Wikipedia); the JPL primary page redirects to a "missing" page.

**10–12.** Citation lines only, per the owner's ruling. Not researched, analysed or sized.

### What this means for us

1. **Cite Micr'Olonys as the nearest prior art.** It also prints a short human-readable bootstrap beside barcode data and restores it with a computer and a scanner. Our difference should be said in one line: one hand-typed decoder (cm2) at every rung, and no emulated machine. Before making any claim about page counts or what its reader types, get the full iPRES 2019 text (doi 10.17605/OSF.IO/9M7QT); a person may have to download it.
2. **Copy the reel-opening order from the Code Vault and Piql.** That means a fixed reference mark, then a human-readable control page (what this is, how many pages, where each part sits), then a table of contents, then the specs, then the decoder. In R2 terms: a front page that names the R2 pages, the typed rung, the archive pages and their order before any data. Also adopt the Guide's honest sentence: a working computer is assumed. The contrast that favours us: their decoder arrives as C source plus the C spec, so the reader rebuilds a toolchain. Ours arrives as about 3,900 typed characters with no toolchain.
3. **Add a known-answer check after the typed rung, if one is not there already.** stage0-posix's phase 0 rebuilds its own seed to prove the seed intact; Voyager's first picture is a known circle. Our version: a short printed vector the loader must reproduce before the reader trusts r.py, which separates typing errors from scan errors. Cheap, and it fits the "typed rung" story.
4. **Report the typed rung the way the bootstrappable-builds world reports seeds.** That world quotes one number (256-byte seed, 280-byte monitor) and makes it auditable. Ours is 29 loader lines and 3,895 typed characters (RECORD 2026-10-20). Publish it as one pinned figure with its exact count rule (what counts as a character, whether whitespace counts), and keep it under regression in `bootstrap/build.py`.
5. **ZPAQ's lesson for the archival layer (r3/arch).** Put the model description in each block and keep the decoder fixed. If strong2 or later CM variants ever differ by more than constants, carry the model parameters in the block header so one typed decoder reads every generation. Adopt ZPAQ's compatibility rule as well: a level-n reader reads any level m ≤ n. This is a format-axis decision under the layout_version discipline, not a decoder tweak.
6. **VXA's argument applies to the stage after the bootstrap only.** Ford: instruction encodings outlast data formats, and an archived native decoder beats no decoder. Once the R3 Python monolith has decoded the archive, the archive may carry decoders for formats that do not explain themselves (images, PDFs). Store those as a fallback and say so, rather than claiming every format is self-describing. His stated risk applies to us too: a fallback that is seldom exercised hides bugs, so the sim lane must exercise it.
7. **Graded readability (Rosetta, Lunar Library) is a design pattern for the outside of the pages.** Eye-readable text on the outer page that leads down to the data tiers costs nothing, and tells a finder what the sheet is before any machine is involved. A presentation choice; no format change.
8. **License note.** If anything from Piql's boxing library were ever borrowed (its LDPC/BCH/RS stack), it is GPLv3 or commercial. libzpaq is public domain.

### Per-work notes

- **LTCB counting rule (VERIFIED).** The ranking is compressed enwik9 plus the zipped decompressor and any files it needs, such as dictionaries. Self-extracting archives count 0 because the decoder is inside the archive. The suffixes mean x = executable, s = source, d = separate decompressor.
- **Hutter Prize (VERIFIED).**
  - Counting: S = the zipped compressor plus the self-extracting archive. With a separate decompressor, S = compressor + 2 × decompressor + archive, and the 2× drops to 1× if they are the same file. Command-line options count too.
  - Limits: about 70,000/T hours on one core (T = Geekbench 5 score, so roughly 50 h), ≤ 10 GB RAM, ≤ 100 GB disk, no GPU, and no outside input such as dictionaries.
  - Recent history: fx2-cmix 110,793,128 (Sep 2024: 441,463 compressor + 110,351,665 archive, about 9.1 GiB RAM, about 64 h decode on a c2-standard-4). Then cmix-lex 109,671,639 (Jun 2026), cmix-obias 108,521,870 (19 Jul 2026), and fx2-cmix-T 100,424,672 (24 Jul 2026, −7.46 %). These 2026 entries postdate my training; I confirmed them with a second fetch of the page asking for the rows verbatim.
- **xz.** The best general LZ, and fast to decode. On text, CM beats it by 20–45 %. On the one pure source tarball measured (linux-1.2.13), even a 169M-parameter LLM beats it by only 29 %. Source code repeats over long ranges, which LZ captures well.
- **bzip2 / ppmd / ppmonstr.** All weaker than lpaq1 on every anchor. ppmd -o16 is 1.17× lpaq1 on samba and 1.105× on Calgary. Not a direction for us.
- **lpaq1 (2007).** The "lite" PAQ: a mix of context orders plus a match model. The decoder is 6,676 B zipped. It runs about 8 s on the Calgary tar on Mahoney's older machine. Memory is 6 + 3·2^N MB, and option -6 (≈198 MB) is plenty for a 9.5 MB input. This is our row's class.
- **zpaq.** The decoder is a fixed virtual machine (ZPAQL), and the model is described inside the archive. The 6.42 decompressor is 4,760 B zipped. On samba, method 5 (3,053 KB) is about 4 % under lpaq1 -6 at a similar speed (~3.8 µs/B), and method 8 is no better. Its default mid.cfg is an order-0–5 chain of indirect context models plus an order-7 match model and a mixer, at 111 MB. Compiling ZPAQL to C++ is 2–4× faster than interpreting it, so an interpreted decoder pays that factor.
- **paq8l / paq8px.** These have type-specific models: text with a word model, sparse, record, x86, images. On source-heavy material paq8l is about 0.86–0.87 of lpaq1 at 30–40× its time. paq8px v217 -12L leads Calgary, Canterbury and Silesia. Its README describes **no source-code-specific model**. The text help comes from stemmers, `english.dic` pre-training and an XML model. Memory at -12 is 24.6 GB. The decompressor is 403 KB zipped source.
- **cmix v21.** Best CM. It builds on paq8l, paq8pxd and fxcm code and adds an LSTM. Compression and decompression take the same time, about 1.5–1.6 KB/s, and it wants ≥ 32 GB RAM. The decompressor is 281 KB zipped. Samba 1.59 MB, but only after precomp. The old v8 without precomp gives 2,591 KB, 0.813 of lpaq1.
- **nncp.** About cmix's size on enwik8, smaller on enwik9. It needs a GPU to be practical, and the decompressor is 629 KB. No source-code numbers are published.
- **ts_zip.** On a 9.38 MB source tar, close to our 9.52 MB, it reaches 0.708 of xz. On enwik8 it reaches 0.556 of xz. The weights (~169 MB at 8 bits) are not counted. For us that would be ~1,500 pages of weights, so it is disqualified.
- **Samba caveat (inference, not measured).** Precomp takes paq8l on samba from 2,754 to 1,886 KB. So samba contains deflate-compressed members that no compressor without recompression can touch, and they pull every samba ratio toward 1. Our tar has none, so samba understates the CM-over-CM gaps for us.
- **Data Compression Explained (Mahoney), VERIFIED.**
  - §2.1.4 has the Calgary-tar table quoted above. The best known Calgary challenge result is 580,170 including the decompressor.
  - §4.1 has order-n pseudocode.
  - §4.3 covers linear and logistic mixing, SSE, indirect SSE, the match model, PAQ, ZPAQ and Crinkler.
  - None of its examples are in Python. As a design reference for adding pieces to strong2 it is the right text.

**Compact context-mixing implementations in high-level languages**

- **Crinkler** (x86 assembler, VERIFIED via DCE §4.3.8): a PAQ1-style linear mix with sparse models. The decoder is about 210–220 B. It skips collision and overflow checks and uses a 256–512 MB table instead. A greedy search picks which models to include. This is the extreme of "decoder size dominates" design.
- **Roadroller** (JavaScript, VERIFIED): logistic CM over sparse contexts up to order 9, hashed from order 3, with a bytewise rANS coder. Parameters are tuned by annealing. The decoder is a minified JS line (size not stated) and is public domain. Latency target is ≤ 1 s for 30 KB, default memory 150 MB. It claims up to 15 % better than the best gzip recompressors and is weaker than DEFLATE on long-range repeats. It is the closest published analogue to our cm2/strong2: a tiny CM decoder in an interpreted language.
- **arithmetic-compressor** (Python, kodejuice, PyPI): arithmetic coding plus PPM and two CM variants (linear and logistic mixing), by its own description minimal. UNVERIFIED: the PyPI page would not load, so this comes from search snippets.
- **cm (10d9e)** (Rust): lpaq-class, with orders 0–6, word, sparse, match model, mixer and SSE. Not Python. Search snippet only, UNVERIFIED.
- No pure-Python or JavaScript port of lpaq1, paq8 or cmix turned up (two searches). Byron Knoll's tensorflow-compress and lstm-compress exist from memory, UNVERIFIED and not fetched.

### What this means for us (ranked)

1. **The decoder must be counted, and at our scale that decides it.** Our payload is ~1.65 MB, about 110 KB per page over 15 pages. The paq8px decompressor (403 KB zipped source) is ≈ 3.7 pages and cmix's (281 KB) ≈ 2.6 pages. That roughly equals or exceeds what they would save (item 2). LTCB and Hutter both count the decoder because of this. The right measure for us is pages(archive) + pages(decoder) + typing effort, and on that measure an lpaq1-class decoder that fits in our 2 R2 pages is near-optimal.
2. **ESTIMATE of the ceiling on our tree, taking strong2 as lpaq1-equivalent.** The cross-check holds: xz/lpaq1 is 1.25 on enwik8 and 1.17–1.21 on samba and Calgary, against our 1.30.

   | Class | Factor on strong2 | Our tree | Saving |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | paq8l / paq8px-v6x class (-8) | 0.85–0.88 | ≈ 1.40–1.45 MB | ~2 pages |
   | paq8px v21x -12L | 0.78–0.84 | ≈ 1.29–1.38 MB | 2.5–3.3 pages |
   | cmix v21 | 0.74–0.82 | ≈ 1.22–1.35 MB | 3–4 pages |
   | LLM class (ts_zip) | 0.70–0.92 | ≈ 1.15–1.52 MB | wide range; disqualified by its weights |

   - The anchors are paq8l/lpaq1 = 0.864–0.873 on samba and Calgary, paq8px -12L/lpaq1 = 0.80 on enwik8, cmix/lpaq1 = 0.74 on enwik8 and 0.813 for cmix v8 on samba.
   - Expect the weaker end. The gains grow with input size (cmix/lpaq1 is 0.74 on enwik8 but 0.66 on enwik9), and at 9.5 MB the models have less to learn from. Part of the text gain comes from English dictionaries and preprocessing that help our Markdown and not our Python.
   - For the LLM row, the linux-tar anchor alone puts ts_zip only about 8 % under strong2. That suggests the source-code ceiling sits closer to us than enwik numbers imply.
3. **Decode time in pure Python at ~100× C (ESTIMATE, 9.52 MB).** Our measured 82 µs/B already matches lpaq1-in-Python.

   | Class | C speed | Pure-Python estimate |
   |---|---|---|
   | zpaq method 5 | 3.8 µs/B | ≈ 1 h |
   | paq8l -8 | ~136 µs/B | ≈ 36 h |
   | paq8px -8 | ~96 µs/B | ≈ 25 h |
   | paq8px -12L | 295 µs/B | ≈ 78 h |
   | cmix | 638 µs/B | ≈ 7 days |
   | nncp / ts_zip | — | impractical: transformer or RNN arithmetic in pure Python costs seconds per byte, i.e. years |

   - The cmix figure is optimistic, because LSTM float loops in pure Python are probably worse than 100×.
   - Memory: paq8px -8 needs 2.4 GB in C, -12 needs 24.6 GB, and cmix ~30 GB. Python arrays would be at least that.
4. **The cheap headroom is upstream of paq8px, and it is specific to source code.** No published compressor carries a source-code model; paq8px's README lists none. Contexts on indentation and column, line start, and identifiers as words for Python could close part of the 12–15 % gap between paq8l and lpaq1 at a few times our current cost. The size of that gain is unmeasured, so it is a candidate to measure, not a promise. The DCE §4.3 pieces (two-layer mixer, chain of indirect context models, SSE stages) are the parts to try. Each 1 % saved is ≈ 16.5 KB ≈ 0.15 page.
5. **zpaq's design suits deep-time archives.** A small fixed decoder (4.8 KB zipped) with the model in the archive header reaches ≈ lpaq1 or 4 % better (samba m5). It is a precedent for keeping the typed decoder generic and putting model choices in the data.
6. **Crinkler and Roadroller show how small a decoder can get.** A 220 B assembler decoder and a one-line JS decoder are achievable because compressor-side search picks a few sparse models and the decoder skips safety checks and uses big tables instead. That matches our typed-rung constraint, and the compressor-side search is free in page terms.
