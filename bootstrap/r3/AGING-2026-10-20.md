## Synthesis (the answer)

# How the R3 rows hold up over 10–20 years of ageing: what we know, what we don't, and how to find out

## 1. The direct answer

**Short version.** Both rows have far more margin than any **uniform** ageing mechanism needs: fading toner, yellowing paper, uniform shrink. The two rows differ in how much **localized** damage they survive, such as creases, rubbing, water lines, mould spots, stuck or lost leaves. Against that, choosing r0700 at q 0.7 mostly spends the q dial, not the code-rate dial.

**The two margins:**

| | c3-k-r0550, q 0.5 (20 pages) | c3-k-r0700, q 0.7 (11 pages) |
|---|---|---|
| Raw cell error tolerated per tile, at 1 % tile failure (simulation) | 4.5–6.2 % | ~3.5 % |
| Fresh c3 black raw error on paper (measured, this record) | 0.05–0.9 % | same |
| Cell-error headroom | ~5–100× | ~4–70× |
| Tiles that may be lost (fountain) | ~50 %, less the reserve | ~30 %, less the reserve |
| Whole pages that may be lost | ~10 of 20 | ~3 of 11 (4 is over budget) |

**Mechanism by mechanism.** Evidence labels: M = measured, S = standard, E = expert opinion, I = our inference.

| Mechanism over 10–20 years | Which margin it uses | What is left | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon-black toner fading | Cell error, negligibly. Uniform, so each tile's pilot thresholds absorb it | Essentially all, both rows | E: CCI, Hopper, PSAP, Nicholson. No measured fade rate exists |
| Uniform paper yellowing | None in effect. Contrast falls a little and pilot thresholds absorb it | All | M on unprinted paper: IPI predicts several centuries for most papers and 64 years for the worst, to just-noticeable yellowing. I for the absorption |
| Uniform or smooth shrink and growth | None. Each tile's own pose fit takes it | All | M: ±0.3 %/day hygroexpansion in this record. I for the absorption |
| Uneven browning: tide lines, tanned edges | Tiles along the line | A: large. B: tight if the same edge band is hit on every leaf | I |
| Creases and folds | Inside each crossed tile, an assumed 0.5–1 mm cracked band gives ~3–6 % raw error. One crease crosses ~26–36 of a page's 840 tiles, about 3–4 % | Crossed tiles: B probably fails them, A is marginal. Page level: both survive a single crease easily | M: Scott 2000 measured ~4× more toner loss for a tighter fold and 5× with toner on the inside. Band width I |
| Rubbing and smear in handled zones (fore-edge, corners) | Cell error at first, then tiles. Thumbs land in the same place on every leaf, so the loss is correlated across pages | B's 30 % is the first budget to go | M: Salesin & Burge saw no damage at a ¼-lb load and separation at 2 lb. I for the correlation |
| Blocking: leaves stuck by heat plus pressure | Whole leaves, or the whole book | A survives ~10 lost leaves, B ~3. A whole-book event kills both | E: toner softens at Tg 50–70 °C (Marcus, Hopper). No measured threshold under book pressure |
| Mould and foxing | Small spots: cell error, absorbed. A ~5 mm spot covers ~38 % of a tile and loses it | Fine below ~65 % RH. Book-wide mould kills both | E: BPG wiki and NARA. I for the cell arithmetic |
| Water | Edge-correlated tile loss, plus toner cracking after swelling and drying | B first | E: Marcus. I |

**Conclusion.** No mechanism we found moves uniform raw error from below 1 % to 3.5 % in dark, dry storage on alkaline paper. That is inference, but every source points the same way. The realistic failures are local or book-wide, and against local loss r0550 at q 0.5 has about 1.7× the tile budget and about 3× the page budget of r0700 at q 0.7. Book-wide events (blocking, a soak, mould across the book) are about equally fatal to both rows. Against those, storage rules and a second copy are the defence, not the row.

## 2. What is not known, and cannot be read from the literature

- **No study measures toner at our scale.** We found nothing on ageing of fused toner features near 0.1 mm: no density loss, edge change or flaking per area, natural or accelerated. The closest is Bogataj 2010, which used mm-scale 2D codes scored readable or not.
- **Long-term adhesion.** Hopper expects the toner film to get "somewhat more brittle" with age, but nobody has measured adhesion over decades. Our three engines' fusing has never been tested, and Scott found "a rather large disparity" between copiers.
- **Crease band width in cells.** Scott reports loss percentages, not how wide the damaged band is. The 3–6 % per crossed tile above rests on an assumed band.
- **Whether ageing errors are diffuse or clustered within a tile.** The simulated rung tolerances assume independent, random cell errors. Clustered damage behaves differently, and the per-tile slot permutation helps only partly.
- **Calendar years from oven time.** No standard converts test days into years. ISO 11798 says its tests "do not correlate to lifetime", and the Library of Congress sealed-tube page says high-temperature data "cannot be extrapolated accurately" to room conditions. The one historical rule of thumb, Rasch's 72 h of dry heat at 100 °C ≈ 18–25 years, is for paper fold strength, not toner.
- **A trap in standard paper ageing.** Ovens run at 80–90 °C, above toner Tg (50–70 °C). Printed sheets aged there can soften, stick or reflow in ways a shelf never sees.
- **Our own paper.** The house stock is not ISO 9706 archival paper (e002). The Library of Congress copy-paper report contradicts itself on strength loss: its summary says significant loss, its conclusion says tensile strength was maintained.
- **Blocking thresholds under book-like pressure.** None are published.

## 3. Recommendation for the R3 row (needs your ruling)

1. **Bootstrap-critical part: c3-k-r0550 at q 0.5.** The ageing evidence argues for spending redundancy on q (tile and page loss) rather than on the code rate (uniform cell error), and this row has the most of both.
2. **Bulk: if page count matters, raise the rung before you raise q.** c3-k-r0700 at q 0.5 comes to about 16 pages by plain arithmetic (11 × 0.7 / 0.5). That is not a capacity run. The rate change from r0550 to r0700 costs little against ageing, because no uniform mechanism uses that margin. Raising q to 0.7 is what shrinks the budget against correlated loss.
3. **The mixed approach (r0550 q 0.5 critical, r0700 q 0.7 bulk) is sound only if the bulk is a separate volume whose loss does not block the bootstrap.** Inside one book the same damage hits both, and the bulk's 30 % budget breaks first. It is acceptable for a best-effort bulk; I would not choose it for anything the recovery chain depends on.
4. **Bigger levers than the row:**
   - a second copy at transposed positions (already in BOOTSTRAP-DESIGN, Appendix A #12), stored apart from the first;
   - storage at about 25 °C or below and 30–50 % RH, never above 65 %, dark, unpressed, never folded, no PVC or glassine;
   - ISO 9706 alkaline paper;
   - moderate toner density rather than the darkest setting (Scott found the heaviest deposit least durable).

## 4. Experiment plan, ordered by information per effort

Every print and every scan needs your go in the session and goes through the `scripts/` wrappers. Every run gets an `experiments/` log entry.

**Step 1. Re-scan the oldest sheets now** (about 1–2 days, no cost).
- **Sentinels:**
  - the e028 and e031 discovery sheets (08-04/05), which carry c2/c3/c4 ladders and need no decode;
  - the e029 and e032 c2 K volumes;
  - e040 B, the only scanned page of the r0700 row.
- **Never-scanned sheets:** e033 A2 and G are c3-r0550 printed 08-18 (the r0550 rung at q 0.3 and q 0.9, not q 0.5). e042 B is c3-k-r0700. Scanning them now fixes their starting point.
- **Controls:**
  - same scanner, dpi, grey mode, and no auto-enhancement;
  - three placements about 9 mm apart, to move the Canon's seams;
  - 24 h conditioning at logged RH;
  - today's instrument run on both the archived and the new capture;
  - never mix old and new captures in one receiver state.
- **Prerequisites (each needs your go):**
  - `scripts/scan.sh` is a stub that asks for Color 16-bit. Its settings must be pinned to the archived ones.
  - `measure/e042-c1-truth/c1_tile_error.py` is hard-wired to c1 at 2400 dpi and needs cell-size and dpi arguments.
- **Expected result:** at three months, no change beyond the scan-to-scan noise. What this step delivers is the method, the noise floor, and the starting point for yearly re-scans.

**Step 2. Mechanical stress on real c3 pages** (about 3–4 weeks; roughly €100 for jig and weights, plus a 50 °C oven).
- **Sheets:**
  - one r0550 q 0.5 page and one r0700 q 0.7 page;
  - a c2-k-r0700 canary page, which sits nearer its limit and moves first;
  - the B/W discovery sheet;
  - on the house stock and on ISO 9706 paper, 3 replicates each.
- **Stresses:**
  - creases: toner inside and outside, along and across the grain, 1/5/20 refolds;
  - rubbing: printed face against the back of a blank sheet at ¼ and 2 lb, 10–1,000 strokes;
  - tape pull (the LoC/NARA preservation-copy test);
  - stacked face to face at about 50 °C and about 70 % RH for 7 days (Hopper's offset test), plus PVC and polyester sleeve sub-arms;
  - 30 °C at 85 % RH for 2–4 weeks;
  - a soak, then drying flat.
- **Readout:** after each stress, the raw error per tile against truth (mean and the 90th and 99th percentile tile) and the tiles certified from one capture against each row's budget. This answers the real question in the receiver's own units.

**Step 3. Heat ageing below Tg, plus a paper comparator** (about 6–8 weeks; equipment estimated at €700–2,500, not sourced).
- **Equipment:** a stable lab oven, an RH/T logger rated for 80 °C, saturated NaNO₃, which gives 65.2 % RH at 80 °C (Greenspan), and a glass or stainless box.
- **Arms:**
  - toner arms at about 40/50 °C and 50 % RH, sheets hung apart, never stacked;
  - ISO 5630-3 at 80 °C / 65 % RH at its own exposures of 24/48/72/144 h, read as a paper test only; anything longer is our own extension.
- **Design:** same sheets as step 2, 3 replicates, controls scanned with every batch.
- **What it can tell us:** whether the bit channel outlives the paper's own strength. It cannot give years.

**Step 4. External ISO 11798 battery.**
- RISE runs ISO 11798 certification for HP; PTS runs the DONot test (folding, rubbing, tape, water, ageing, light). Cost: a quote is needed.
- Ask for the aged specimens back and decode them ourselves. Their CIELAB pass/fail cannot see effects at the cell scale.

**Step 5. Naturally aged prints** (a day of scanning).
- **Material:** your own 1990s–2000s laser printouts, ideally ones whose source file still exists, scanned at 4800 grey.
- **Measure, against a re-render:** stem width, edge raggedness, voids, cracks along old folds, offset onto facing pages.
- **Limits:** this bounds which failure modes occur, not their rate. Toner, fusers, paper and storage history all differ from ours.

**Step 6. A natural-ageing shelf, started with step 2's print run.**
- Extra copies stored in several places: a dark drawer, a warm attic, sleeves.
- Re-scanned yearly under step 1's controls.
- This is the only source of real decade-scale data, and it arrives in a decade.

## Research: standards

# Standards and accelerated-ageing methods for dry-toner prints on paper: what they test, and what they can say about 10–20 years of ageing

## Bottom line

- **No published study measures what this project needs.** I found no data on how small cells of fused black toner on office paper hold up over 10–20 years, either naturally or in accelerated tests.
  - The closest standard is ISO 11798. It tests whole patches and sets colour and lightness tolerances, roughly ±8 ΔL* in Table 1 of the 2023 edition.
  - The closest measured study is the Image Permanence Institute's Arrhenius study of paper yellowing. It used unprinted papers.
- **Expert sources agree that carbon-black toner on alkaline paper is a very stable image.** The Canadian Conservation Institute, the Image Permanence Institute and a Xerox toner chemist all say so. The same sources name the real risks:
  - the paper itself;
  - how well the toner is fused to it;
  - blocking or offset under heat and pressure;
  - cracking at folds;
  - offset onto PVC.
- **No standard turns test days into calendar years, and the standards say so.** The "3 days ≈ 25 years" figure comes from Rasch's 1929 dry-heat work at 100 °C, later run at 105 °C by TAPPI. It does not come from ISO 5630-3 (80 °C, 65 % RH), which states no years at all. Library of Congress and European Commission on Preservation and Access reviews judge absolute year conversions unreliable.
- **A specific problem for toner:** toner resins soften at about 60–70 °C (their glass-transition temperature, Tg). The standard paper-ageing temperature of 80 °C is above that, so it may trigger failure modes, such as blocking, that would never happen at room temperature (details in section 4).

## 1. ISO 11798: permanence and durability of writing, printing and copying on paper

Source: the 2023 second edition and the 1999 first edition. For both I read the free preview text, which runs as far as the requirements clause. The test-method clauses (clause 6) are behind a paywall.

**What it measures.** The 2023 edition grades seven properties:
- visual image colour strength and appearance;
- lightfastness;
- water resistance;
- transfer of the recorded image;
- abrasion resistance;
- resistance to heat;
- the effect of the recording on the paper's mechanical strength.

Specimens must be printed on ISO 9706 or ISO 11108 paper, conditioned at (23 ± 1) °C and (50 ± 2) % RH, and printed from the standard's own templates. Printer settings must be reported, because "print adjustments and other settings on a printer may have major impact on the permanence" (ISO 11798:2023, 4.3, note).

**Pass/fail criteria, 2023 edition (standard's requirements):**
- **Colour and lightness:** after the light, water and heat tests, each base colour from a printer must stay within ΔL\*, Δa\*, Δb\* of ±8 and ΔE\*ab ≤ 10. Yellow is allowed Δb\* ±15 and ΔE ≤ 15 (Table 1).
- **Ghost printing:** traces of characters on the white areas that could interfere with the content fail the specimen (5.3.2).
- **Abrasion:** now measured as the percentage change in L\* before and after abrasion (definition 3.4).
- **Methods referenced:** xenon-arc light per ISO 4892-2; abrasion with abrasive wheels per ISO 9352; tensile per ISO 1924-2; folding endurance per ISO 5626.

**Pass/fail criteria, 1999 edition (standard's requirements):**
- **Density:** black from copiers and laser printers needed a minimum ISO visual density of 0.90, and 0.80 after light exposure.
- **Heat:** density must still meet Table 1 "after having been kept in accordance with 6.7 for 12 days" (4.7).
- **Wear:** the image's retention after abrasion must exceed 0.80 of a reference ink's. "Flaking of part of the image" must not occur at the initial abrading step (4.6).
- **Paper strength:** tensile energy absorption may drop by at most 10 % (4.8.1).

The temperature and humidity of the 6.7 heat test are not in the open preview, and I could not verify them. I believe it uses moist heat, but that is unverified recollection.

**What it says about dry toner (standard's text):**
- "Images produced from dry or liquid toner are also susceptible to ageing problems" (Introduction).
- The testing "does not correlate to lifetime of documents". Documents that pass "can be stored for a long time … probably for several hundred years" (2023, Introduction).
- It excludes documents "where the information contents are influenced by small colour changes" (Scope).

**Who tests to it (organisational claims, not data):**
- HP's site says RISE (Research Institutes of Sweden) certifies HP cartridges against ISO 11798. The models HP lists are ink cartridges, the claim is worded "designed to meet", and the site gives no test values.
- In Germany, PTS (Papiertechnische Stiftung) and Ugra offer ISO 11798 testing. Ugra's page lists the criteria but no conditions.

**My inference for this project.** A ±8 L\* tolerance on a solid patch is far coarser than what the receiver cares about. The receiver sets a threshold per tile from that tile's own pilots, so uniform darkening or yellowing of that size is absorbed. ISO 11798's abrasion, transfer and folding tests are closer to the failure modes that would break 0.127 mm cells.

## 3. Paper standards: ISO 5630, ISO 9706, ISO 11108, ANSI/NISO Z39.48

**ISO 5630-3:1996 — moist heat at 80 °C and 65 % RH.** I read the preview by OCR.
- **Rationale:** the rate of cellulose degradation "increases about 25 %" when RH rises from 60 % to 70 %. Paper in the test should therefore have the same moisture content as in natural ageing, and 80 °C / 65 % RH was chosen "after studying the ageing of many papers".
- **Apparatus:** the chamber must hold (80 ± 0.5) °C and (65 ± 2) % RH.
- **What it leaves open:** it "does not specify the tests to be made", and it gives no duration-to-years equivalence.

**The other parts of ISO 5630** (from preview and listing pages, not full texts):
- Part 1: dry heat at 105 °C.
- Part 2: 90 °C and 25 % RH.
- Part 4: 120 or 150 °C, for electrical-insulation papers.
- Part 5: per a reseller listing, elevated temperature at 100 °C for printing and writing papers. I did not read it, so treat this as unverified.
- Part 6: per a reseller listing, nitrogen dioxide (atmospheric pollution). Not read.

**ANSI/NISO Z39.48-1992 (R2009).** I read the full text.
- **Uncoated paper:** pH 7.5–10; machine-direction tear index ≥ 5.25 mN·m²/g; alkaline reserve ≥ 2 % CaCO₃ equivalent; ≤ 1 % lignin (kappa number ≤ 7).
- **Claim:** such paper "will last several hundred years".
- **Notable omission:** it deliberately excludes "tests after artificial aging", because the criteria for unaged paper "appear to be adequate predictors".
- **Printing:** it names "the use of printing inks that do not adversely affect paper permanence" as a separate concern, outside the standard.

**ISO 9706:1994** (values from a Seoul National University summary, Youn & Cho, 2008; I have not read ISO 9706 itself):
- tear ≥ 350 mN for paper ≥ 70 g/m²;
- alkali reserve ≥ 0.4 mol/kg;
- kappa number ≤ 5;
- pH 7.5–10.

**Current editions:** ISO 9706:2025 and ISO 11108:2025 are new second editions. ISO 11108 (archival paper) adds fibre-composition and folding-endurance requirements to ISO 9706's. I could not see whether the 2025 editions change any ageing test.

**Limits of these standards:** they apply to unprinted paper. As the Xerox chemist Hopper (2004) notes, "compliance with these standards unfortunately does not ensure" that a print on such paper will be permanent.

## 4. Converting test durations into years

**Where "3 days ≈ 25 years" comes from.**
- Rasch (1929, US National Bureau of Standards) defined a "permanency factor": folding endurance after 72 h at 100 °C, as a percentage of the initial value.
- Wikipedia, citing Porck (2000), gives "72 hours at 100 degrees Celsius is considered equivalent to 18–25 years of natural aging".
- A search-result summary says TAPPI later moved the test to 105 °C, giving the "3 days ≈ 25 years" phrasing. I did not open the TAPPI source, so this step is unverified.

The equivalence therefore belongs to dry heat at 100–105 °C, not to ISO 5630-3. I found no published years-equivalent for 80 °C / 65 % RH. The Library of Congress gives only relative lifetimes: about 12 days at 80 °C / 65 % RH for a single sheet of one acidic book paper, measured by fold endurance.

**Criticism (expert opinion and measured negatives):**
- **Shahani (Library of Congress, 1995).** Bansa and Hofer aged a naturally aged paper further at 50–95 °C and found "no correlation between natural and accelerated aging". Bansa (1992) called conclusions from such tests potentially "deceitful". Stroefer-Hua (1990) held that Arrhenius plots for paper "are not straight lines", so the high-temperature mechanism differs from ambient ageing.
  - Measured in Shahani's own lab: paper in stacks, or sealed in PET film, aged about twice as fast as free-hanging single sheets, because acidic degradation products accumulate. Test geometry matters as much as temperature.
- **Porck (European Commission on Preservation and Access, 2000).** Current tests "do not allow for a reliable determination of the rate of paper degradation in absolute terms". Meaningful use is limited to relative, qualitative statements. (I read search summaries and catalogue entries, not the report.)
- **Counterpoint.** Zou, Uesaka and Gurnagul (Cellulose 3, 1996) report that their kinetic model, valid where first-order Arrhenius conditions hold, predicted the natural ageing of lignin-free sheets "with a statistical confidence". I read the publisher abstract only; it is paywalled.
- **Long-term check in progress.** The Library of Congress's 100-year natural-ageing programme started in 2000 with 15 papers stored at 10 institutions. Samples are pulled in 2000, 2003, 2008, 2013, 2018, 2028 and onward to 2098, explicitly to test whether accelerated ageing predicts natural ageing. The page I read (updated 2013) reports no comparison results yet.

**A toner-specific trap (expert source plus my inference).**
- Hopper (2004, 28 years at Xerox Research Centre of Canada) states that toner resins have Tg "between 60 and 70°C". Fusers run at 150–180 °C. It is "unwise … to store a printed document at temperatures above 60°C under compression", especially duplex pages where toner touches toner.
- **My inference:** ISO 5630-3's 80 °C, and the 80–85 °C top temperatures of IPI's series, sit above toner Tg. Results there can include softening, blocking or offset that never occurs at room temperature, which breaks the single-mechanism assumption behind Arrhenius extrapolation. A toner-on-paper Arrhenius series should keep every temperature below about 60 °C and avoid compression, or treat the 80 °C points as a separate failure mode.

## 5. Measured and expert evidence on toner prints specifically

- **IPI yellowing study** (Nishimura, Burge, Bigourdan, Gordeladze, AIC Book and Paper Group Annual 30, 2011). Measured result:
  - **Samples:** 28 digital-print paper types, including office paper for black-and-white electrophotography, tested **unprinted**.
  - **Conditions:** pre-conditioned at 21 °C and 20/50/80 % RH, sealed in foil bags, incubated at 55, 65, 70, 75, 80 and 85 °C for 1–94 weeks.
  - **End-point:** a Status A blue density rise of 0.05, i.e. just-noticeable yellowing.
  - **Results:** document and digital-press papers yellowed less than photo papers. Spread within a category was sometimes larger than spread between categories. Most materials were predicted to "last several centuries before noticeable yellowing". The weakest material was predicted to last 64 years at room conditions.
  - **Limits:** paper only, no toner image; the yellowing end-point is cosmetic, not a readability limit.
- **Hopper (IS&T Archiving 2004).** Expert and industry account:
  - Toner is 85–95 % polymer resin and 5–15 % pigment. Black is carbon black, "very stable in almost all environments". Colour pigments can fade, yellow especially.
  - The fuser is "likely the component that has the most significant impact on document life". Cold-pressure-fixed prints are "believed not to be archival".
  - Some inexpensive alkaline photocopy papers "have endured for 30 years" without yellowing. This is an observation, not a controlled study.
  - Vendors' in-house tests include crease tests and vinyl offset (about 50 °C, about 70 % RH, up to 7 days under load), scored 0–5.
  - Toners containing styrene can be plasticised by PVC sleeves.
- **Canadian Conservation Institute Technical Bulletin 22** (Grattan, 2000). I read the catalogue abstract only. Black-and-white copies with carbon toner on alkaline paper "form very stable records, but colour photocopies do not". The bulletin describes a full-sheet test for toner adhesion.
- **IPI Consumer Guide (2004).** Electrophotographic prints are "generally less sensitive to high temperature and humidity" than photographic prints, but more susceptible to scuffing because the image sits on the surface. Some electrophotographic colours "may bleed onto an adjacent sheet", especially against PVC.
- **University of Illinois PSAP guide** (expert opinion). Black laser prints "tend to be very stable and permanent". It notes "toner offsetting and image loss due to high heat", and advises against polyester sleeves for laser prints.
- **Light, ozone, NO₂ (IPI DP3 project).** These studies included electrophotographic prints. Secondary summaries say ozone was dosed at 5 ppm, 25 °C, 50 % RH for two weeks, and that dye-based inks faded most. I did not read the results for toner.

No published data found on:
- loss of toner adhesion over decades of natural ageing;
- dimensional change of toner relative to paper at the scale of 0.1 mm features;
- legibility or bit error rate of fine black toner features after accelerated ageing.

## 6. What this means for c3-k-r0550 and c3-k-r0700, and how to get real samples

All of this section is my inference; none of it is measured.

**What should not matter much:**
- Uniform darkening or yellowing, and colour shifts within ISO 11798's ±8 L\* tolerance, are absorbed by per-tile pilot thresholds.
- Uniform shrink or growth is absorbed by fitting each tile's pose.

**What could matter, if anything does:** local losses. These are toner flaking, abrasion and crease damage (ISO 11798's abrasion and folding tests, ISO 18947), and offset or blocking in stacks (ISO 11798 transfer test). They would show up as clustered cell errors or lost tiles, which the fountain layer (needing about 50 % or 70 % of tiles) is built to tolerate. Uneven humidity cycling is a further risk; Shahani found single sheets degraded faster under cycling humidity.

**How to get evidence without waiting 20 years:**
1. **Commission ISO 11798 on the actual combination** (this engine, this toner, this paper) at RISE, PTS or Ugra. Ask for the aged specimens back and decode them with our own receiver, reporting raw cell error before and after. CIELAB pass/fail will be blind to cell-level effects.
2. **Run our own sub-Tg Arrhenius series**, for example 40/50/60 °C at 50 % RH, sealed or free-hanging, per ISO 18924 practice. Add ISO 5630-3 (80 °C, 65 % RH) as the paper-side comparator, keep unaged controls from the same print, and measure decoder raw error per tile. Physical runs need the owner's go per the hardware rules.
3. **Read naturally aged material.** Archived prints from this record can be re-scanned periodically; the earliest certified volume (e016) dates from 2026-07, so it has only about three months of natural ageing. Separately, the decoder's cell-truth instruments could be applied to the black solids and fine text of 1990s–2000s office laser prints on alkaline paper, to measure toner retention at about 0.1 mm after 20–30 years of natural ageing.

# How black laser toner on office paper ages: published evidence and what it means for c3 cells

## Bottom line

- **No published long-term study measures what I need.** I found no natural-ageing or accelerated-ageing study that measures what matters for 0.127 mm black cells: loss of optical density at small feature size, change in edge sharpness, or flaking per unit area of office laser prints over 10–20 years. I also found no condition survey of 1980s–2000s office laser printouts held in archives.
- **What does exist:**
  - Image Permanence Institute (IPI) and DP3 accelerated tests, which are mostly on colour and photo materials and mostly published as abstracts.
  - One 14-year natural-ageing study, of laser labels kept in jars of liquid preservative.
  - Manufacturer-side test descriptions from a former Xerox toner chemist.
  - Archive guidance that is mostly expert opinion.
  - Two fold and rub studies that quantify toner loss mechanically.
- **The consistent picture.** Carbon-black toner fused onto alkaline copy paper is chemically very stable. The paper yellows slowly; IPI predicts decades to centuries at room conditions. The realistic failure modes for a 20-year archive are mechanical and storage-related:
  - toner loss at creases and folds;
  - abrasion between sheets;
  - toner sticking to facing pages or to vinyl when stacked under pressure and heat;
  - mould above about 65–70 % RH;
  - water.
- **Implication for the receiver (my inference, not tested).** Uniform density drift and yellowing should sit well inside the pilot-normalised thresholds. A fold line, by contrast, can put a band of errors through every tile it crosses.

## 1. The toner image itself: colourant and binder

**Carbon black is not expected to fade.**

- *Expert opinion and guidance:*
  - The Canadian Conservation Institute bulletin (Grattan 2000) concludes that carbon-toner black-and-white copies on alkaline paper are "very stable records", while colour copies are not. I read only its catalogue abstract; the full PDF did not render for me.
  - The Library of Congress asks for toner with "carbon black pigment" in preservation copies, and for proper fusing.
  - The University of Illinois Preservation Self-Assessment Program (PSAP) rates black-and-white laser prints low-risk and colour laser prints higher-risk.
  - The National Park Service Conserve O Gram 19/4 (1993) recommends only toners containing carbon black, in stable resins.
  - IPI's 2004 consumer guide says electrophotographic images are "reasonably stable" because they are pigment fused in a polymer binder. It also says gaseous pollutants are not a problem for them.
- *Measured, accelerated:*
  - **Ozone.** IPI's DP3 project ran 5 ppm ozone, 25 °C, 50 % RH, for two weeks (1,680 ppm-hours). The authors equate this to about 21 years of nominal indoor exposure, assuming reciprocity. Electrophotographic prints were more resistant than inkjet, and the summary advises treating them "like traditional prints" (Nishimura 2011).
  - **Nitrogen dioxide.** 5 ppm for four weeks. Black colorants were comparatively resistant, so text-only documents should be very fade-resistant, though the paper may yellow (Burge et al. 2011, abstract only).
  - **Pre-dosed prints in storage.** Follow-up storage of ozone- and NO2-pre-dosed prints, black-and-white electrophotography included, showed further yellowing and colorant change after several hundred days (Gordeladze et al. 2012, abstract only). The abstract does not break out electrophotographic results.

**The binder: stable, possibly more brittle, softens near 60 °C.** Hopper (2004, IS&T Archiving; the author spent 28 years at Xerox Research Centre of Canada) is the most specific source. All of the following is expert statement, not measurement:

- Styrene-acrylate, styrene-butadiene and polyester toner resins "do not degrade significantly with time". The only likely change is that the films become "somewhat more brittle".
- Toner resins have a glass transition of about 60–70 °C. He advises against storing prints above 60 °C under compression, especially duplex prints where toner touches toner.
- Plasticisers from PVC soften styrene-based toners. This is the documented "vinyl offset" problem; polyester toners resist it better.
- Wax in oil-less toners migrates to the film surface and changes how the toner bonds to paper.
- Cold-pressure-fixed prints are "believed not to be archival", because rubbing removes the toner.

NPS 1993 adds a claim (stated as "an indication", with no data) that ageing photocopies can keep transferring their image onto adjacent sheets, especially under pressure and heat, and that plasticised vinyl can lift the image off the page.

## 2. Adhesion, abrasion, folding: the mechanical failure modes

**Natural ageing, 14 years, in liquid (the only multi-decade-scale measured study found).**

- *Setup:* Zala, Pentcheff and Wetzer (2005) printed labels in March 1989 on an Apple LaserWriter Plus (Canon LBP-CX engine, 300 dpi), on five 100 % cotton-rag papers. The labels were immersed in 70 % ethanol or 10 % formalin until March 2003.
- *Natural-ageing result:* At 60× magnification the damage "was so slight" that the treatments could not be told apart. All letters remained readable, with "no bleeding or fading".
- *Rub test:* They then rubbed the labels 50 cycles on a TAPPI ink rub tester (0.9 kg load, glass faces) while wet. Damage was mostly chipping and cracking of toner, with few missing sections.
- *Effects:* Paper type had no significant effect, and neither did microwave heating. Acrylic coating and ethanol (rather than formalin) reduced damage.
- *Conflicting anecdotes:* The paper also cites one museum report of labels "for over ten years with no apparent loss" in ethanol, against reports of desktop-printer toner flaking off after weeks to months in preservative.
- *Relevance:* This is wet storage, much harsher chemically than a dry archive, and 300-dpi text, not cells.

**Rub threshold (measured, abstract only).** Salesin and Burge (2013) tested digital-press documents on a Sutherland 2000 rub tester.

- A ¼-lb load, which simulates page turning and sorting, produced no noticeable damage "even after many hundreds of cycles".
- A 2-lb load separated the technology and paper combinations.
- The abstract does not give electrophotography-specific numbers.
- The earlier DP3 abrasion paper (Salesin et al. 2008) included electrophotographic prints and established the methods (Ugra and Sutherland tests; density, gloss, ΔE and smudge measures). Its abstract gives no electrophotography-specific results.

**Fold and crease (measured, most relevant to cell loss).** Scott, Gleisner and Dahlke-Bauman (2000, USDA Forest Products Laboratory, TAPPI Recycling Symposium) used a rolling-fold tester on copier output and measured toner loss as newly exposed white pixels in 300-dpi scans. They validated that measure against weight loss.

- Narrowing the fold gap from 1 to 0.64 mm increased toner loss about 4×. Loss kept growing with the number of cycles.
- With toner on the inside of the fold, loss was 5× that with toner on the outside.
- Folds in the machine direction lost more than cross-direction folds. The fractures were evenly spaced lines parallel to the fold.
- The heaviest toner deposition was the least durable, and the lightest was also worse than the intermediate levels.
- Durability differed between 20 % and 50 % RH. The authors attribute this to paper stiffness and toner brittleness.
- The three copiers tested differed widely.
- Mechanism: the toner layer first fractures, then peels.
- One non-uniform specimen lost 13.1 % on one half and 29.4 % on the other.
- This is a fold-durability test, not ageing. It shows the scale of loss a hard crease can cause.

Hopper describes manufacturers' crease test the same way: a poorly fixed layer "breaks along the crease", and better fusing narrows the exposed band.

**Adhesion test (requirement).** The Library of Congress specifies the National Archives (NARA) tape-pull test for preservation copies (Technical Information Paper No. 5). A copy passes if no text, "not even letter outlines", transfers to the tape.

**Stacking and offset (manufacturer test conditions, not a result).**

- Hopper describes the vinyl-offset test as about 50 °C and about 70 % RH for up to 7 days under load, scored 0 to 5.
- The same sandwich test is used for toner against paper and toner against toner. He reports that "many toners fail" where toner touches toner, and some fail where toner touches paper, particularly on papers surface-sized to improve toner adhesion.
- IPI's blocking and ferrotyping study (30 °C, 90 % RH, 7 days) found digital prints generally less prone to blocking than photographic prints, per the Nishimura 2011 summary. That summary does not name electrophotographic results separately.

## 3. Humidity, mould, dimensions

- **Bleed (measured, abstract only).** Salesin, Burge, Nishimura and Gordeladze (2010) exposed inkjet, electrophotographic and dye-sublimation prints to high humidity for 2 and 4 weeks. They measured ΔE on a checkerboard, line-width change parallel and perpendicular to the paper feed, and text readability. The DP3 summary (Nishimura 2011; 30 °C at 75 % or 85 % RH) reports that only dye-based inkjet bled significantly. Other processes "won't bleed" but may block or grow mould. I found no published electrophotographic line-width numbers.
- **Mould (guidance).** NARA: "RH should be kept below 65%", with 45–55 % RH and 18–20 °C as targets.
- **Dimensions.**
  - The only source I opened gives no magnitude: hygroexpansion is "several times greater" across the machine direction than along it (Etherington and Roberts, conservation dictionary).
  - I found no study of permanent shrinkage of printed office paper with age.
  - This repository's own channel facts record paper hygroexpansion of about ±0.3 % per day.

## 4. The paper: yellowing and strength

**IPI Arrhenius study (measured; the best quantitative ageing data found).** Nishimura, Burge, Bigourdan and Gordeladze (2011) tested 28 unprinted papers, including standard office paper for black-and-white electrophotography as a control.

- *Conditions:* 55–85 °C at 20, 50 and 80 % RH, incubated for up to 94 weeks.
- *Endpoint:* a rise of 0.05 in status A blue density, described as clearly noticeable.
- *Predicted life:* the weakest material reaches the endpoint after 64 years at room conditions; most materials "should last several centuries".
- *Variation:* differences between products sometimes exceeded differences between categories.
- *Interpretation:* the authors attribute the yellowing institutions actually see to light and air pollutants, not heat.
- *Limits:* the samples were unprinted, so the test says nothing about the toner film.

**Library of Congress copy-paper study (measured).** PRTD (2014) aged copy papers per ASTM D4714 at 90 °C and 50 % RH for up to 56 days. Under an assumed doubling of reaction rate per 10 °C, the report calls that equivalent to 19.6 years.

- The copy papers started strongly blue (b* of −9 or lower) because of brighteners. They crossed into yellow only near the end.
- Brightness fell nearly linearly over the test.
- All copy papers lost significant strength, but all were judged permanent by the study's definition.
- 100 % recycled paper performed significantly worse than the other copy papers.

**Natural ageing anecdote (expert statement).** Hopper notes inexpensive alkaline copier papers that "have endured for 30 years" without yellowing.

## 5. Forensic dating literature

- Document-dating work concentrates on ink solvents. In the toner study I opened, the methods are said to be used mainly in the first two years.
- A 2024–25 Raman study (Kochemirovskaya et al., abstract only) dates the **paper under the toner** across sheets from 2016–2022. It treats the toner as a shield and reports no toner change.
- I found no forensic study documenting physical or optical change of fused toner over years. That is weak negative evidence that nothing changes visibly.

## 6. What this implies for c3-k-r0550 and c3-k-r0700 (my inference, untested)

1. **Contrast drift is the small risk.** Yellowing of +0.05 blue density, the IPI endpoint at decades to centuries, is small next to the black/white separation. The per-tile thresholds set from 100 pilot cells absorb global drift. Toner fade on carbon black is not expected.
2. **Folds are the large risk.** A tile is 64 × 0.127 mm = 8.1 mm on a side. A crease that cracks a 0.13–0.5 mm band (my assumption, since Scott gives loss percentages, not band widths) wipes 1–4 cell rows, or 1.5–6 % of a tile's cells.
   - That approaches r0550's 4.5–6.2 % budget, before adding the 0.05–1 % fresh error.
   - It exceeds r0700's roughly 3.5 % budget for any wider band.
   - The per-tile slot permutation spreads the band across the codeword, which helps the LDPC. The fountain then covers lost tiles: one fold line across A4 crosses about 26–36 tiles, and the 50 % (r0550) or 70 % (r0700) tile requirement leaves large slack.
   - Repeated flexing, or toner on the inside of the fold, multiplies loss by the factors Scott measured (4×, 5×).
3. **Rub and stacking:** sliding sheets under weight, duplex toner-on-toner contact at warm temperatures, and vinyl sleeves are all documented offset and abrasion paths. Each removes or smears toner locally, which shows up as clustered cell errors.
4. **Dimensional change:** uniform shrink is absorbed by the per-tile pose. Machine-direction versus cross-direction anisotropy is several-fold, so the pose model should allow independent x/y scale. I have not checked whether it does.
5. **Margin:** fresh c3 error is 0.05–1 % against 3.5–6 % tolerated, so about 5–100× headroom against diffuse degradation. Against localised mechanical damage the relevant margin is the fountain's tile slack, which is larger for r0550 (q = 0.5) than for r0700 (q = 0.7).

## 7. Getting aged samples ourselves

No literature closes the gap, so the evidence has to be made. Options, cheapest first:

- **Natural time series.** Re-scan already-printed, already-decoded sheets of this record (e016 from 2026-07, e029, e036/e037 sheets H and I) once a year on the Canon at a fixed dpi, and keep the raw cell error per tile against ground truth. This is the only true ageing data obtainable, though it is slow.
- **Found old prints.** Scan the owner's own 1990s–2000s laser printouts at 2400 dpi and measure edge profiles and isolated-dot density against a fresh print from a similar engine. This gives indicative numbers only, since the engines, toners and storage history are unknown.
- **Accelerated heat ageing, with a caveat.** Paper-ageing protocols (ASTM D4714 at 90 °C; IPI's 55–85 °C) run above the toner's 60–70 °C glass transition (Hopper). The toner then softens and flows, a mechanism room-temperature ageing never triggers. Toner-relevant heat ageing should stay below about 55 °C, with sheets hung apart rather than stacked.
- **Mechanical stress tests:**
  - LoC/NARA tape pull.
  - Sutherland-style rub at ¼-lb and 2-lb loads.
  - Controlled creases, toner inside and outside, along and across the machine direction.
  - Hopper's stack-offset sandwich at 50 °C and 70 % RH for 7 days, both against paper and toner to toner.
  - 30 °C at 85 % RH for 2–4 weeks for humidity.
  - Decode each stressed sheet and count tiles lost.
- **Standards to align with:** ISO 11798, the permanence and durability of writing, printing and copying on paper. It covers light, wear, heat and water, and grades abrasion by L*. The full text is paywalled, so I read only its scope pages. ISO 9706 covers permanent paper.

## Research: mechanisms

# How ageing would eat into the two candidate R3 archive rows (c3-k-r0550 at q 0.5, c3-k-r0700 at q 0.7)

## Bottom line

- **Even, page-wide fading is not the 20-year threat for black carbon toner on alkaline paper.** I found no evidence of any mechanism, in dark storage on decent paper, that would push raw cell error from the 0.05–0.9 % measured fresh in this record up to 3.5–6 %. That would be a 4- to 70-fold rise.
- **Localized damage is the threat.** Creases, rubbing, water tide lines, mould and foxing, pages blocking together, and insect grazing wipe out whole tiles in patches. They spend the fountain's tile-loss budget, not the LDPC's cell-error budget.
- **The two rows differ much more in tile-loss budget (about 50 % vs about 30 %) than in cell-error budget (about 4.5–6 % vs about 3.5 %).** The ageing evidence therefore favours row A, or more generally spending redundancy on q rather than on the code rate (my inference).
- **Storage rules can stop almost every mechanism.** Keep the book cool, at moderate humidity, dark, flat and unpressed. Never fold it.
- **One finding bears on "can we get aged samples": standard accelerated paper ageing runs above the toner's softening temperature.** Standard ovens run at 80–90 °C, while toner binders soften at about 50–70 °C. So an ordinary oven test on printed sheets would create an artefact of its own.

Labels used below: **[M]** measured result, **[S]** requirement of a standard, **[E]** expert or guideline opinion, **[I]** my own inference.

## 1. What the channel does with each kind of damage (from the repo)

- **Geometry.** A c3 cell is 0.127 mm, so a 64-cell tile is about 8.1 mm square. The R3 test rows are `@35x24`, which is 840 tiles per A4 page (`bootstrap/r3/README.md`).
  - Row A: 20 pages ≈ 16,800 tiles, of which about 8,400 are needed.
  - Row B: 11 pages ≈ 9,240 tiles, of which about 6,500 are needed.
- **How the fountain spreads tiles over pages.** Each page carries an exact share of every block's tiles, shuffled within the page by the keystream. Losing whole pages, up to the fraction 1 − q, is therefore a deterministic recovery tier. Losses inside a page fall at random across blocks (`docs/HOLO-DESIGN.md` §7.2 and F8).
  - Damage that hits the same place on every page (bottom-edge water, wear at the fore-edge corners) costs the same fraction of every block. It is affordable exactly while that fraction stays below 1 − q minus the reserve. **[I]**
- **What the receiver absorbs.**
  - Each tile sets its own thresholds from its 100 pilot cells, so contrast drift that changes slowly across 8 mm is absorbed.
  - Each tile fits its own pose, so uniform shrink, and anything smooth at the tile scale, is absorbed.
  - Neither absorbs damage at the cell-to-millimetre scale inside a tile: a spot, a crease, a smear, or a fold that kinks the tile's geometry. **[I]**
- **What the repo's design already assumes.** It puts toner Tg at about 55–65 °C with a storage ceiling below 35 °C (ideally below 21 °C), dimensional change of about 0.3–0.8 % across large humidity swings, mould risk rising above about 65 % RH, and black as the most durable plane (`docs/DESIGN.md` §2.5). None of those numbers carries a citation there.
- **What the deep-time design already prescribes** (`docs/BOOTSTRAP-DESIGN.md`):
  - K-only carbon toner.
  - Blank versos, against blocking.
  - At least 20 mm gutter and 10 mm trim margins.
  - Duplicates placed at transposed positions (Appendix A #12).
  - A recopy directive.
  - IR reading demoted to an advisory (#20).
- **Measured hygroexpansion [M].** About ±0.3 %/day (Channel facts). ±0.5 % is 3.6 cells cumulative across a 720-cell R2 span. At 0.5 % across one 8.1 mm tile the error is about 40 µm, about 0.3 cell, which the per-tile pose takes up. **[I]**

## 2. Mechanism by mechanism

### 2.1 Toner density loss (fading)
- **Evidence:**
  - Black laser prints "tend to be very stable and permanent", with black toner described as carbon pigment in resin [E] (PSAP, Univ. of Illinois).
  - A conservation case study rates black xerographic copies as having good to excellent light stability, "depending on the quality of the paper support". It names the resin binder, not the carbon, as the limiting factor [E] (Nicholson 1989).
  - RIT's permanence tests (heat, light, humidity, pollutants, abrasion, water) found electrophotographic prints generally more resistant than offset lithography [M; I read only the abstract] (Burge, Farnand & Frey 2012).
  - No published fade rates for black toner were found.
- **Which margin:** in principle the cell-error margin, but the rate is negligible. **[I]**
- **Absorbed?** Yes. A uniform loss of density is a contrast change, and the per-tile thresholds take it.

### 2.2 Paper yellowing and browning (contrast loss)
- **Evidence:**
  - The Library of Congress aged six papers at 90 °C / 50 % RH (ASTM D4714) for up to 56 days. Four were alkaline, lignin-free copy papers; two were reference papers.
  - All papers moved toward yellow. But the copy papers' pH did not change and they kept their tensile strength: "paper yellowing was not necessarily an indication of chemical change" [M] (LoC PRTD 2014).
  - Optical brighteners lose fluorescence under light. In one study of photographic papers, most samples kept at least half of it. Brighteners can account for about one-fifth of reflectance in the blue [M] (Connors-Rowe, Whitmore & Morris 2007).
- **Which margin [I]:**
  - Uniform browning lowers paper reflectance mostly in the blue. The toner stays far darker, so contrast survives and per-tile thresholds absorb it.
  - Yellowing is strongest in blue. A K receiver that reads the **red** channel of an RGB capture, rather than luma, is a cheap lever against it on the receiver side; this is untested.
  - Browning that is uneven at the tile scale matters: tanned edges, light-struck margins, tide lines. If it varies across 8 mm it moves cells across the threshold → cell-error margin within a band; a sharp edge → lost tiles.
- **Absorbed?** Uniform browning yes; tide lines no.

### 2.3 Abrasion, flaking and smearing (handling, page turning)
- **Evidence:**
  - Rub tests (Ugra, Sutherland) on inkjet and electrophotographic prints [M; abstract only] (Salesin et al. 2008).
  - A ¼-lb rub simulating sorting caused no noticeable damage after hundreds of cycles. A 2-lb load, simulating pulling sheets from large stacks, did separate printer/paper combinations, and colorant smear was the most objectionable damage [M; abstract only] (Salesin & Burge 2013).
  - "Prints with improperly fused toner are especially vulnerable to friction", and prints stacked directly on each other abrade [E] (Marcus 2018).
  - Fusing quality is engine-dependent; three copiers showed "a rather large disparity" [M] (Scott et al. 2000).
- **Which margin [I]:**
  - Toner removed from black cells lightens them, and smear darkens neighbouring white cells. Both are cell errors concentrated where hands go: fore-edge corners and the lower outer quadrant.
  - Inside a tile the damage is either mild and spread out (cell-error margin) or heavy and patchy (tile loss).
  - Because thumbs land at the same place on every leaf, the loss is correlated across pages. It lands entirely on row B's 30 % budget.
- **Absorbed?** Partly. Thresholds absorb a general lightening; smear lines are not absorbed.

### 2.4 Cracking at folds and creases
- **Evidence:** a rolling-fold tester on copier paper found [M] (Scott et al. 2000):
  - Narrowing the fold gap from 1 to 0.64 mm raised toner loss about 4×.
  - Toner on the inside of the fold lost 5× more than toner on the outside.
  - Toner lost more when the fold made the paper's machine direction bend, and RH (20 % vs 50 %) changed durability.
  - Failure was "cohesive fracture in the first couple of cycles, followed by adhesive failure (peeling)".
  - The highest toner deposition was the least durable.
  - Folding an electrophotographic print can crack or delaminate the toner [E] (Marcus 2018).
- **Which margin [I]:** a crease is a line about 0.5–1 mm wide, 4–8 cells across 64 cells, so roughly 250–500 cells per tile it crosses. If half of those read wrong, that is about 3–6 % raw error per tile. That is at row B's limit and near row A's. The fold also kinks the geometry, which breaks a single per-tile pose. One crease across a page costs one row or column of tiles (about 3–4 % of the page), which both rows survive.
- **Absorbed?** No. The tiles crossed are lost. Fine at small counts.

### 2.5 Blocking and offset to facing pages (heat + pressure)
- **Evidence:**
  - Xerographic binders are "vulnerable to blocking to adjacent surfaces with heat or pressure", and PVC plasticiser softens toner [E] (Nicholson 1989; repeated by PSAP).
  - Toner polymers have Tg of about 122–158 °F (50–70 °C). The closer the storage temperature gets to Tg, the likelier blocking is; glassine interleaves cause blocking and toner lift [E] (Marcus 2018, citing Galliford 2001).
  - No published threshold for blocking under book-like pressure was found.
- **Which margin [I]:**
  - The ghost lands on the facing page. With blank versos it is harmless.
  - Toner pulled *off* the data recto when sheets are separated takes out patches, or the whole page if it is welded.
  - Blocking is a **whole-page / whole-book** event, because heat reaches every leaf of a book. That is fatal to both rows if the whole book blocks.
- **Absorbed?** No.

### 2.6 Foxing and mould
- **Evidence** (BPG Wiki "Foxing", AIC) [E, with cited studies]:
  - Causes are disputed: fungal, metal-catalysed, or both.
  - Fungal growth is unlikely below about 70–75 % RH (Arai 1987; Beckwith et al. 1940).
  - Spots range from small "bullseye" spots, through copper-related spots up to about 5 mm, to "snowflake" spots that can be inches across.
  - Papers with more calcium or magnesium carbonate are less likely to fox.
  - A secondhand report of rust-brown "shadowing of the text" on page backs, attributed to ferrite or iron-oxide toner, came from a DistList post citing Grattan's 2000 CCI guidelines; I did not read the bulletin [E] (Juergens 2013).
- **Which margin [I]:**
  - A spot under 1 mm darkens about 60 cells → about 0.7 % of a tile → cell-error margin, fine.
  - A 5 mm spot covers about 1,500 cells (about 38 % of a tile) → the tile is lost.
  - Snowflake spots or mould colonies → several tiles.
  - Humid episodes act across the whole book and position-correlated, but at random locations, so the losses are spread → tile-loss margin.
- **Absorbed?** Small spots by LDPC; larger spots no.

### 2.7 Water damage
- **Evidence:**
  - Toner areas "are prone to cracking or delamination when the paper is exposed to high humidity or moisture and then dried", because the paper swells and contracts beneath the toner [E] (Marcus 2018).
  - Swelling of paper is a known toner-fracture driver, citing Vander Wielen et al. 1999 [M, as cited in Scott et al. 2000].
  - I found a 14-year study of laser labels immersed in fluids but could not read its text, so I do not rely on it.
- **Which margin [I]:**
  - Tide lines (sharp bands of darkening) → tiles lost along the line.
  - Cockling → warp at the centimetre scale, mostly absorbed by per-tile pose. Warp at the tile scale is not, and flatbed glass only partly flattens it.
  - Toner cracking over the wetted area → raised cell error across that area.
  - Wet pages that stick and are then pulled apart → blocking-like lift.
  - Water usually enters from one edge (a shelf flood, or a leak onto the top edge), so the loss is correlated in position across leaves.
- **Absorbed?** Cockling partly; the rest no.

### 2.8 Dimensional change (permanent shrinkage)
- **Evidence:**
  - Hygroexpansion is about ±0.3 %/day [M, repo, Channel facts].
  - Irreversible shrinkage happens on the first humidity cycles and depends on the drying restraint at manufacture. A study of freely and restrained dried sheets reported only qualitative differences in its abstract [M, abstract only] (Vonk et al. 2023).
  - I found no percentage published for copy paper.
- **Which margin:** essentially neither. A uniform or slowly varying scale is taken by per-tile pose; the channel already sees ±0.5 % in fresh captures. **[I]**
- **Absorbed?** Yes. This is the mechanism the receiver is best placed against.

### 2.9 Insects
- **Evidence:** silverfish damage ranges from "grazing over the top layer of paper in a rasping fashion" to eating through it; one recorded case attacked only a white ink on a cover [E] (Smith 2016). Nothing published on toner specifically.
- **Which margin [I]:** surface grazing lifts the top layer with the toner on it → patches → tile loss. Usually local and uncorrelated across pages.

### 2.10 Light exposure
- **Evidence:** carbon black's light stability is above (2.1). Brightener loss and paper yellowing were covered in 2.2.
- **Which margin [I]:** a closed book receives light only at its edges and on whatever leaf lies open. Edge tanning falls in the 10 mm trim margin and on edge tiles. A leaf left open on display tans evenly over the page (absorbed) or with a sharp shadow edge, a few lost tiles.

## 4. Ranked: what breaks each row first [I]

The cell-error budgets differ by only about 1.3–1.7× (4.5–6 % vs 3.5 %). No uniform mechanism found here comes near either, so **the ranking is driven by the tile-loss budget**: about 50 % for row A vs about 30 % for row B.

**Row B (c3-k-r0700, q 0.7, 11 pages)** — first failures, in order:
1. **Damage correlated in position across every leaf**, above about 25–30 % of each page's tiles. Examples: water wicked from the bottom edge, thumb wear at the fore-edge, a mould bloom along the outer third. Each takes the same fraction of every block, and 30 % minus the reserve is a small budget for an edge-plus-corner pattern.
2. **Loss of whole leaves.** Four of 11 pages lost (36 %) is over budget. A few leaves torn, blocked or lost from a failed binding is ordinary for a used book.
3. **Heavy wear plus creasing on a well-handled book**, pushing crossed tiles past 3.5 % raw.
4. **Blocking of the whole book** (shared with row A).

**Row A (c3-k-r0550, q 0.5, 20 pages)** — first failures:
1. **Book-wide events:** blocking from hot, pressed storage; a full soak followed by sticking; mould across the whole book. These are mostly all-or-nothing for both rows.
2. **Correlated damage above about 45–50 %**, which takes a severe flood or rot.
3. **Losing more than about 10 of 20 leaves.**
4. **Cell-error mechanisms:** only for a badly fused print that is then abraded or creased over most of each page.

**A consequence worth a ruling:** the rung (code rate) and q are separate dials. The ageing evidence argues for spending redundancy on **q**, against localized loss, rather than on the rung, against uniform loss. A row like c3-k-r0700 at q 0.5 would be about 16 pages by plain arithmetic (11 × 0.7 / 0.5); that is my arithmetic, not a capacity run.

## 6. On "can we get aged samples or studies"

- **Published studies:** the evidence on black toner over decades is qualitative and in conservators' hands. No published natural-ageing measurement of toner adhesion or contrast was found, and nothing at our feature scale.
- **Standard accelerated ageing has a trap for toner [I]:** paper ageing ovens run at 80–90 °C (LoC used 90 °C / 50 % RH). That is above toner Tg (50–70 °C), so oven-aged prints would reflow, spread dots and stick — artefacts that do not happen on a shelf. Age sheets hung apart and read the result as a paper-only test.
- **The question can be answered on our own channel without ovens [I]:** standard physical stress tests (crease per ASTM F1351, rub per Sutherland/Ugra, wetting and drying), applied to printed c3 sheets from our actual engines, then re-decoded. The output is raw cell error and tiles lost per stress, in the receiver's own units.
- **Natural ageing starts now:** re-scan the record's own sheets each year. e016 was printed 2026-07-10 and later sheets have dates; captures already on disk give the baseline.

# Ageing of the R3 rows: evidence we already have, and an accelerated-ageing design

## 0. Bottom line

- **Measured (repo):** a c3 page's fresh K raw cell error is 0.05–0.9 %. The r0550 rung tolerates 4.5–6.2 % and r0700 about 3.5 % (BSC, simulation). That margin is 4–100× in raw error, before the fountain absorbs whole-tile loss (about 50 % for q = 0.5, about 30 % for q = 0.7).
- **No published data found** on toner-on-paper bit channels at the 0.127 mm cell scale after ageing. The nearest study (Bogataj et al. 2010) used 2D codes of roughly mm-scale modules. Black laser codes were unchanged after 6 days at 80 °C / 65 % RH (see §3).
- **Expert/standard view:** carbon-black toner is stable, so the risks are paper and adhesion: abrasion, offset/blocking, creases. ISO 11798 says its own tests "do not correlate to lifetime", and the Library of Congress review says high-temperature data cannot be extrapolated reliably to room temperature.
- **My inference:** accelerated ageing cannot give "years". It can give a dose-to-failure margin per rung, compared with the dose at which the paper itself becomes unusable. Only a natural-ageing shelf started now gives real decade data, in a decade. The 3-month-old sheets in the record give the first point and, more usefully, the method and the noise floor.

## 2. Accelerated-ageing experiment

### 2.1 What the standards and literature give

- **ISO 5630-3:1996** is moist heat at 80 °C / 65 % RH for "printing and writing papers" (catalogue listings; full text not read, so its durations are not quoted here).
- **LoC, Shahani 1995:**
  - Moisture during ageing is essential; dry and humid ageing follow different chemistry.
  - The ISO 9706 committee chose 80 °C / 65 % RH.
  - On one offset paper, 12 days at 80/65 gave the same relative lifetime as 6 h in a 10-sheet sealed bag at 100 °C.
  - Critics (Bansa; Stroefer-Hua) doubt any natural/accelerated correlation.
- **LoC sealed-tube project:** sealed-tube ageing gives the closest chemical match to natural ageing, but high-temperature data "cannot be extrapolated accurately" to room conditions.
- **ISO 11798:2023 (standard):**
  - Rates lightfastness, water resistance, transfer, abrasion, heat resistance and the effect on paper strength.
  - Applies to images on ISO 9706/11108 paper.
  - Uses xenon arc (ISO 4892-2) and abrasive wheels (ISO 9352), with ΔL* via ISO 13655.
  - Says toner images "are also susceptible to ageing problems".
- **Carbon black (expert opinion):** IPI's consumer guide calls EP prints "reasonably stable" and says they are less sensitive to heat and humidity than dye images. It warns of bleed onto adjacent sheets, especially with PVC sleeves.
- **Abrasion (measured method):** IPI used Ugra and Sutherland rub tests, rubbing the back of one sheet over the image of another. Sutherland was the "more severe".
- **Storage guidance:** Illinois PSAP rates black laser prints low risk and lists offsetting and heat loss. It advises against polyester sleeves and recommends 2–18 °C and 35–50 % RH.
- **Measured, wet storage:** a 14-year study of laser-printed museum labels in preservatives (Zala et al. 2005) found all still legible. Earlier anecdotes report desktop-printer toner flaking or rubbing off; heat and pressure govern adhesion.
- **Measured, 2D codes:** Bogataj et al. (2010) printed codes on an HP Color LaserJet 3550, then applied Xe light, 6 days at 80/65, and water immersion. Black laser codes were the most readable before and after. Ageing left the readability of all CMYK prints "mostly unchanged" (coarse modules, reader pass/fail).

### 2.2 Sheets

Print each set per (engine × paper):

1. The HCAL-R1 discovery B/W sheet (c2/c3/c4 and 2×3 ladders).
2. A `c3-k-r0550` q 0.5 volume page.
3. A `c3-k-r0700` q 0.7 volume page.
4. **A c2-k-r0700 canary page.** c3 may show no change, which bounds nothing. c2 sits nearer its threshold (fresh 2–7 % raw), so it moves first; per the channel facts, c2 whites are the day/density canary.

Papers: the house 90 g/m² ECF office stock (e002: "NOT ISO 9706 archival stock") and one ISO 9706 permanent paper (pH 7.5–10, ≥ 20 g/kg CaCO₃ reserve, kappa ≤ 5, LoC table).

Engines: start with the M281 at home. Add the engine the archive would actually use, the Canon iR-ADV or the AltaLink, as the second engine. Only change toner state between batches, never within one.

### 2.3 Stress arms

Use 3 replicate sets per arm. Each set is repeated-measures: remove, condition 24 h at 23 °C / 50 %, scan, return.

| arm | dose | rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Control | dark, cool, sealed; scanned with every batch | removes scanner and day drift |
| Moist heat | 80 °C / 65 % RH, 0/3/6/12/24 days (48 if no change) | ISO 5630-3 condition; paper chemistry |
| Dry heat | 80–90 °C, < 10 % RH, same days | Shahani: a different mechanism; a check on toner reflow and adhesion |
| Light | behind window glass, months, with dose logging | ISO 11798 uses xenon-arc exposure (ISO 4892-2); for K the paper is the likely loser (inference) |
| Rub | Sutherland-style: printed face against the back of a blank sheet under weight, 10/50/200/1000 strokes | IPI's method; the likeliest real handling failure |
| Fold | one centre fold, then 1/5/20 refolds | a crease cuts a line of tiles; measures tiles lost per crease |
| Blocking/offset | stack face-to-face under weight at 50 °C (and at high RH), 1–4 weeks; plus PVC and polyester sleeve sub-arms | IPI and PSAP flag offset and sleeve transfer; also tests the facing page for ghost marks |
| Humidity cycling | 30↔80 % RH daily, 4 weeks | cockle and non-uniform distortion against per-tile pose |
| Water (optional) | 10 min immersion, dry flat | toner is water-insoluble (HP); tests paper distortion |

Moist heat at home:

- **Saturated NaNO₃ gives 65.22 ± 1.1 % RH at 80 °C** (Greenspan 1977, Table 2). KI gives 60.97 % and NaCl 76.29 %.
- Use a glass or stainless container with sheets on a rack above the salt, inside a temperature-stable lab oven, with an RH/T logger rated for 80 °C.
- Conservation Physics warns that saturated-salt RH drifts upward when water is absorbed and misbehaves when temperature is unstable; condensation on opening is a real risk.
- **Inference:** a kitchen oven is not stable enough.

### 2.4 How to read the outcome

For each sheet and timepoint:

1. **Raw cell error per tile at truth-fitted pose** (genie and blind thresholds), reported as the mean and the 90th/99th-percentile tile. The worst tiles decide tile failure, because ageing damage clusters (creases, rub tracks), while the rung thresholds assume BSC.
2. **Certificates from one capture**, decoded with `--complete` on a fixed receiver tree: the fraction of tiles certified against the fountain's budget (about 50 % may be lost at q 0.5, about 30 % at q 0.7).
3. **Contrast:** paper-white and K levels.
4. **The per-tile error map,** to see whether damage is local or uniform.

Report the **margin** = rung threshold minus the 90th-percentile tile error, as a function of dose, against the control sheets of the same day. The key comparison (inference) is whether the dose at which r0700 or r0550 fails exceeds the dose at which the paper itself fails. ISO 11798 measures paper strength by tensile and fold endurance. If the bit channel outlives the paper, the paper is the binding constraint, and the answer is ISO 9706 stock, not a lower rung.

## 3. Naturally aged samples available now

**Sources:**

- The owner's own decades-old laser printouts: theses, manuals, tax files.
- Ideally pages whose source file still exists, so the page can be re-rendered at the printer's dpi and aligned. That gives a truth-referenced edge error, close to a genie cell error.
- University archives and records offices holding 1980s–90s photocopies and laser prints (inference).

**What to measure** (Canon 4800 grey, same controls as §1.3; ISO/IEC 24790 defines a standard set of text print-quality attributes, not read here):

- stroke width of font stems vs the re-render
- edge raggedness (std of edge position along strokes)
- solid density and voids
- stray specks and foxing per cm² on white
- toner cracking along old folds
- offset ghosts on facing pages
- toner lift where sleeves or tape touched

**Why it transfers only partly:**

- ISO 11798 itself says old-document studies are "of limited use" for modern materials.
- Toner chemistry, fusers and paper have all changed. Pre-1990s papers were often acidic, today's are alkaline (inference).
- Storage history is unknown, and there is no cell-level truth.

**Inference:** these samples bound the failure modes (adhesion loss, cracking, offset) better than the rate.

**Start a natural-ageing shelf now.** Print the §2.2 set in extra copies. Store them dark and cool, in an office drawer, in a hot attic, and in sleeves. Rescan yearly under the §1.3 controls. A three-month data point is also owed immediately from the e028, e031, e029, e032 and e040 B sheets.
